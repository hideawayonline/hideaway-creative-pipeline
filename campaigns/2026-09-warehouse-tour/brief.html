The read

The list already told us they were worried

On 30 August the ownership update went to the full file — 118,347 recipients, subject line “Hideaway is still trading and we are here to stay”, signed Chris Dobson, CEO. It opened at 57.7% against our usual 34–38%. People were paying close attention.

Two other numbers from that send tell the rest of the story.

Roughly 860 people unsubscribed off that one send.

Read together: a large part of the list opened because they were worried, read a statement, and got no proof. Some of them left.

A letter is a claim. A warehouse tour is evidence.

This is the follow-up chapter — the same message, shown instead of said. It's a trust email, not a promo. Revenue is the second-order effect, not the ask.

What the letter actually said

The ground the copy has to stay on

The copy references the letter directly, so it can't contradict it or overclaim past it. Verbatim from the sent template:

Following the recent media coverage regarding the liquidation of Platinum Investment Group Pty Ltd (the company which previously operated Hideaway Online), the business is transitioning into a phase of new ownership, during this time orders continue to flow in and dispatch daily from our warehouses.

Chris Dobson, CEO · 30 August 2026

Three constraints fall out of that, and two of them changed the draft:

Present tense only

The previous operating entity was liquidated, so any claim of unbroken historical continuity is unsafe. “Everything you've ever ordered came from this building” is cut.

Warehouses, plural

The letter says warehouses. Don't imply one site is the whole operation.

Chris signed it, not Steve

A first-person callback only works over Chris's signature. The draft says “we wrote”, so it holds either way.

The safe ground is exactly what the letter already claimed publicly and what the footage independently shows: stock on shelves, orders dispatching daily, real staff at real desks, product coming off the line. Stay there and the email is both true and strong.

The framing

We told you we're here to stay. Here's the building.

That single line does three jobs at once: it closes the loop the letter opened for the people who wanted more than words, it re-warms the worried-but-still-subscribed before they churn too, and it earns an easy, low-commitment click.

One structural change

Host the tour on our own site, not Instagram

Click-to-open is sitting at roughly 1.1% across recent campaigns. People open and don't click. Sending them to Instagram makes that worse — the session ends on someone else's platform.

Build hideaway.online/pages/inside-the-warehouse with the reel embedded at the top, two or three lines underneath, and the range as a product grid below it. Same emotional payoff, but the click lands somewhere that can convert and we keep the traffic.

If the page can't be built in time, ship anyway — point the CTA at the collection and use the reel as a linked GIF. Don't hold the send for it.

Linking the reel

Instagram is the fallback, not the destination

The permalink is instagram.com/reel/DdA5Ndzit-M — posted 8 September, 211 likes, 25 comments, running 1 min 43 sec. Both links in the draft now point at it, so nothing in the email is dead.

It's still worth building the on-site page, for three reasons:

Desktop hits a wall

A logged-out visitor clicking through to a reel gets Instagram's login overlay instead of the video — a wall where we promised proof.

The session ends there

They watch, they scroll, they're gone. No product grid, no path back, nothing to buy.

The trail stops at the click

Klaviyo logs the click and that's all we ever learn.

On mobile with the app installed it deep-links fine, so it isn't broken — it just leaves most of the value on the table for a page that takes about an hour.

Getting the file

Or from the app: your own post → … → Download. Then upload to Shopify → Content → Files and embed with a native <video> tag — not an Instagram embed, which drags in their script and can show a login prompt.

On the length: 1:43 is long for an email click-through, and for a promo that would be a problem. Here it's probably fine — someone worried about whether you're still trading will watch the lot, and the length is part of the proof. Cut a 15-second version for the hero if there's time, but don't hold the send for it.

Lead with A, not B — deliberately. The callback belongs in the body, where only people who open will reach it. Roughly 42% of the file never opened the 30 August letter; for them a subject line about still being here creates a doubt they didn't have. A is warm and safe for that group, and the people who did read the letter still get the full payoff in the first line. Test B on a holdout rather than leading with it.

Both deliberately plain. Our best recent subjects — “Midnight”, “I said there'd be no round three”, “Dad's sorted. Now you.” — work because they're short and confident. No emoji on this one; the message is sincerity.

The hero image

The production floor, not the front door

The strongest frame in the reel isn't the shopfront — it's the production floor: stainless blending tanks, an automated filling and labelling line, staff working in hairnets, stock and trolleys either side.

The difference matters. A shopfront proves a building exists. A working production line proves a business operates — which is the actual question in customers' heads after the 30 August letter. It's the most reassuring image in the whole clip.

A bench reads as a spare room. Tanks and a filling line read as a manufacturer.

It changed one line of copy

The bullets used to say “The bench where the fragrances get blended and bottled by hand.” There is no bench on screen. Running that caption over that image is a small credibility gap in an email whose whole job is credibility, so the bullets now name what's actually there — the tanks, and the line that fills, caps and labels.

What it deliberately does not say

The absences are the point in a message like this. Nothing about the liquidation or the previous operating entity. Nothing about new ownership — the letter covered that, and this doesn't reopen it. No claim of historical continuity, cut deliberately. No “hand poured”, “handmade” or “handcrafted”. Nothing about rewards, points, pricing or offers.

Three things actually need a reviewer's attention. Everything else is either already published in the 30 August letter, or verifiable by watching the video.

  1. “Same perfume” — formulations unchanged through the transition?
  2. “Same building” — the letter says warehouses plural; is the filmed site the one that ships?
  3. Paragraph 3 — do all four things named appear in the footage?

This audit is not the sign-off. It's a first pass to make the sign-off fast. A marketing email touching trading status during a liquidation and change of ownership should still be cleared by whoever cleared the letter.
